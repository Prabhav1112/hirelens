INTERVIEWER: Tell me about the payments microservice redesign you mentioned on your resume.

CANDIDATE: Sure. So actually the redesign was mostly driven by our tech lead — I was one of
three engineers on it. My specific piece was rewriting the retry/idempotency logic for failed
payment webhooks. I used a Redis-backed idempotency key store so we wouldn't double-charge
customers on retries.

INTERVIEWER: You said on your resume you "single-handedly architected" the migration to
microservices. Can you walk me through the architecture decisions you made?

CANDIDATE: Honestly "single-handedly" is probably too strong a word, my manager and I worked
on the high-level design together in a few workshops, and then I implemented the messaging
layer using Kafka. I picked Kafka over RabbitMQ mainly because we already had ops experience
with Kafka on the data team, so it reduced operational risk.

INTERVIEWER: How do you approach mentoring junior engineers?

CANDIDATE: I try to do a lot of pairing rather than just code review comments. For example
one junior engineer on my team was struggling with async/await patterns in Python, so we
spent two sessions pairing on a real bug in our webhook consumer until it clicked for him.

INTERVIEWER: Can you describe a time you disagreed with a teammate?

CANDIDATE: Yeah, during the microservices migration a teammate wanted to use synchronous
HTTP calls between services for simplicity. I pushed back because I was worried about
cascading failures under load. We eventually did a small load test together which showed
the synchronous approach falling over at about 3x expected traffic, and he agreed to switch
to the async Kafka approach.

INTERVIEWER: Why do you want to leave your current role?

CANDIDATE: I've grown a lot at Zenith but I'm looking for a team with a stronger data
engineering culture, since I want to grow more in that direction.
